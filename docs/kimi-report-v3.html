00 · The call

effbench does not have a numbers problem. It has a framing problem, and framing is design. The five moves below are the whole mandate; everything after this section is implementation.

  • Indict, don't defend. The product already measures the user's own 198 tok/s peak. Show it, own it, label it unchecked — and subordinate it. The user stops envying the internet's number the moment they realise they own that number too, and know what it's worth.
  • Every speed wears an adjective. Checked vs unchecked becomes the product's vocabulary — on every number, in every view. The non-technical user walks away with a question that wins arguments: "yeah — but were the answers right?"
  • Kill the cross-rig "typical." An n=1 soak from a different rig is a lie with a footnote. Replace it with Your Normal (the user's own history, n≥3) and demote the soak to a labelled appendix. Answer "how do we know what's typical?" truthfully: we can't yet — here's what we can know.
  • Every run pays out. Today only record-breaks get ceremony; ~90% of runs end in silence. The Garage makes every run a win or a lesson: champion vs challenger, recipe notes, negative results framed as knowledge purchased.
  • Hand the user the argument. A Claims Translator ("seen a 200 t/s screenshot? check it") and a copy-ready Verdict Receipt turn private reassurance into public advocacy — with provenance attached, always.
01 · Diagnosis

Why 62 feels bad — and why that's a design bug, not a math bug

The user's words: "I am slightly concerned that despite all the model tuning, we still get low numbers… How do we know what typical is?" Six failures produce that feeling. None is in the measurement.

1. The product explains; it never argues.

The equation chip 67 × 92% = 62 is arithmetic. The user doesn't doubt the math — they doubt whether the metric matters, because the screen never says why it matters, never says what the 200 tok/s screenshots actually are. Explanation convinces engineers. Feelings need an advocate.

2. The comparison vacuum.

We ship almost no context, so users import context from the least truthful source available: social media screenshots. If you don't supply the frame, the internet supplies its own. The fix is not borrowed reference data (we have none that's truthful) — it's making the user's own data do the arguing, and giving them a tool to interrogate foreign claims.

3. The n=1 band is a lie with a footnote.

"One rig's soak, not a crowd's average" is a good label on a bad idea. A cross-rig "typical" derived from a single machine will be misread as a verdict no matter how it's captioned — the user literally did misread it, asking "how do we know what typical is?" Labels don't launder thin data. effbench's one asset is being believed; this band spends it.

4. Ninety percent of runs end in silence.

The ★ badge fires only when a run beats every prior run. Tweakers run dozens of losing experiments — each one purchased information, none of it rewarded. The product's emotional surface is mostly switched off. A benchmark that only celebrates records teaches users to chase records — the exact vanity loop we exist to kill.

5. The forever-sweep is a spinner in a suit.

An in-flight segment that sweeps forever claims progress it doesn't have. An instrument that guesses teaches users to distrust instruments. We have (or can earn) real duration data — show elapsed, show an ETA only once history justifies it.

6. The numbers have no adjectives.

The UI renders "62 tok/s" and "198 tok/s" as if they were the same kind of thing. They are not. One is checked — every answer graded. One is unchecked — speed with no idea whether the words were right. Until the screen never lets them look alike, the user will keep comparing our smallest truthful number against the internet's largest hollow one.

02 · The thesis
Don't defend the smaller number.
Indict the bigger one.

The reference rig posts a raw peak of 198 tok/s — measured, this run, on the user's own hardware. That is the same species of number as every "200 t/s" screenshot on the internet: generation-only, best-case, zero answers graded. The user already owns the number they envy. They just needed someone to show it to them wearing the right label.

So the post-run moment stops being a scoreboard and becomes a verdict: a sentence first, the number as evidence, and the user's own peak displayed beneath — hollow, dashed, unchecked. The dopamine the brief asks for doesn't come from inflating 62. It comes from the user realising, viscerally: I could post 198 too. I know what it's worth. Mine is the one that survives scrutiny. That feeling is superiority through truth — and it's the only feeling this brand is allowed to sell.

One consequence, stated plainly because the brief asked me to argue: the feedback says "we DO NOT want the user thinking they are getting lower numbers." Agreed — but the method is not to hide the gap or soften the comparison. The method is to put 198 on screen, owned and indicted. Hiding it leaves the user alone with Reddit. Showing it gives them the win.

03 · Design laws

Seven laws, binding on every screen, report and terminal line

L1
The sentence is the headline; the number is the evidence.Every result opens with a verdict in plain language. The hero numeral proves it. Never a bare number asking to be compared to a screenshot.
L2
Every speed wears an adjective.Checked (answers graded) or unchecked (not). No bare tok/s anywhere — UI, report, terminal, share text. Mint is reserved for checked.
L3
No number without provenance. Visible, not hovered.Every figure carries a one-line source caption from the trust ladder (§09). Tooltips don't count. If it prints, it prints with its source.
L4
Your rig is the only fair fight.Comparisons start with your own history. Everything external is labelled reference, community, or claim — never "typical," never charted on the same axis as your measured data.
L5
Every run pays out.Win → ceremony. Lose → knowledge, logged and named. First run → baseline, the start of the story. No neutral outcomes, no silent runs.
L6
Failures are targets, not stains.A failed task is named, with the grader's reason, framed as the next thing to beat. Amber, not red. Red is reserved for product errors.
L7
Truth is the brand.When we don't know — typicality, other people's rigs — the product says so in words, then shows what we do know. Confidence about our own ignorance is the feature.

The low-pass state never cheers and never scolds — it redirects the tuning instinct at the model. Tone-matched quips die here; specificity is the personality.

05 · The run screen

True progress, live stakes

Keep the segmented rail and fading task rows — they're good. Kill the forever-sweep. The run screen's job is to make 15 seconds (or 4 minutes) feel like watching evidence accumulate, not waiting for a spinner.

STATIC SPEC · annotatedrun in progress
task 8/12 · structure · json_schema · elapsed 0:41 · usually 0:52 passed 7/7 so far
BUILD NOTE — segments: resolved-pass solid mint, resolved-fail amber, in-flight steady 35% mint (no sweep, no motion — a pulse is permitted, static under reduced-motion), upcoming hollow. Status line is one row, wraps on phone. "usually 0:52" appears only with ≥2 prior runs of this suite on this setup — an ETA we haven't earned is the sweep in a new hat. Live pass tally gives early dopamine and makes waiting informative; it is the single cheapest emotional upgrade in the product.

06 · The Garage — the tweaker's reward loop

Champion vs challenger. Every run pays out.

The past-runs table becomes the Garage: a tuning journal where the best run per suite is the Champion — pinned, with its recipe displayed as the thing to beat — and every other run is a challenger with a user note ("quant Q4→IQ4_XS", "ctx 8k→32k", "draft off"). The ★ badge dies; its trigger graduates into a ceremony with three states. Toggle them:

INTERACTIVE · ceremony statesthe garage
BUILD NOTE — ceremony card renders once per run, immediately after the Verdict. Champion state persists as a card above the run panel. Notes field placeholder: "what changed? e.g. quant Q4_K_M → IQ4_XS". Spec-decode acceptance (98.5–100%) lives here as a recipe diagnostic line — demoted from the hero cluster, where it was noise for the non-technical and a brag about the wrong thing.

The Verdict Receipt — the brag, weaponised

The user wants to post something. Give them something that wins. One button — Copy verdict — puts this on the clipboard:

My local AI: 62 checked tok/s — 11 of 12 tasks graded correct, wall-clock, cold start.
Raw peak: 198 tok/s, unchecked — same as the screenshots.
effbench · measured on my machine · nothing left it.

provenance: every figure in the receipt is measured · the receipt never carries reference, community or claim data (L3/L4) · terminal gets the same text

The receipt converts the old brag line's job into the user's own voice, in the product's vocabulary. Every shared receipt is an ad for the frame: checked vs unchecked.

07 · The Claims Translator

"I've seen people talking about 200 t/s." — Check it.

This is the direct answer to the user's paragraph. Not a number to fight the screenshot — a tool that shows what the screenshot is missing. Enter any claim; the translator does arithmetic, truthfully labelled as arithmetic. Try it:

LIVE PROTOTYPE · fully functionalclaims translator
A speed claim with no pass rate isn't a number. It's an ad.
illustration · arithmetic on an unverified claim · their pass rate was not measured · your pass rate (92%) measured this run
BUILD NOTE — input clamped 1–100000; invalid → "enter their claim, not your doubts."; >2000 appends "ambitious." to the caption. Claim numbers never appear on any chart (L4) — this module is the only place external claims may exist, and they exist here as arithmetic, not data.

The translator converts insecurity into scepticism — the exact emotional jujitsu the brief demands. It also teaches the frame better than any paragraph could: the user discovers, with their own hands, that the missing pass rate is where the truth lives.

09 · The trust ladder — provenance as a system

"How do we know what typical is?" Truthfully: we can't yet. Here's what we can know.

Every number in the product carries one of these labels — visibly, always (L3). The ladder is also printed in settings and in the report, so the system explains itself.

tierlabel (exact)what it isrules
T1measured this runLive data from the current benchmark.The only tier allowed in the hero.
T2measured · your history · n={k}Past runs on this machine, this setup.Basis of Your Normal (n≥3) and earned ETA (n≥2). Never blended with other tiers.
T3reference soak · one rig · n=1The dev rig's soak (120–200 gen-only).Appendix only. Never charted against T1/T2 on one axis. Never called "typical."

T5unverified claimNumbers the user types in (screenshots, forum posts).Translator only. Never charted, never stored as data.

Your Normal — the real answer to "is my setup good?"

  • Locked (n<3): "Your normal unlocks after 3 runs on this setup. One run is a measurement; three is a pattern."
  • Open (n≥3): p10–p90 of your wall, gen-only and pass rate, this rig, this model string: "Your normal · 5 runs: wall 63–71 · gen-only 138–145 · pass 89–96% · this run: inside."
  • Outside: "This run is outside your normal (wall 54 vs 63–71). Something changed — check your recipe notes." This is the fit line's soul, rebased onto data that's actually comparable.

Community tier — what to collect, when, and how to label it meanwhile

Do not collect anything silently, and do not promise "typical." Ship the export schema in P2, opt-in per run, off by default:

{
  "effbench": "0.7.0",        // product version
  "suite":    "quick-v1",     // frozen suite id — comparability lives here
  "model":    "as reported by server, raw string",
  "quant":    "parsed from model string, or null",
  "gpu":      "user-typed, or null",
  "eff": 62, "wall": 67, "gen_med": 141, "pass": 0.9167,
  "ts": "2025-…"
}// never: prompts, outputs, machine identifiers, IP-derived anything

until n≥30 per (suite × gpu-class) cell, community data displays as individual dots labelled T4 — a crowd of named points, not a fake smooth band. Meanwhile the label says what is true: "community-reported · unverified · early days." The truthful empty state is the design.

14 · If even §5 were negotiable

What I'd do next, with the gloves off

  • Signed result files — the end of unverifiable screenshots. Hash prompts + outputs + grades + suite id into every export. The Verdict Receipt becomes a proof, and a new trust tier appears above community: verified peer. The 200 tok/s screenshot dies of embarrassment in public, at scale, and effbench is the weapon that kills it.
  • Seasons for the frozen prompts. "Frozen forever" is right within a season and wrong across years — models will overfit the 48. Annual suite seasons: frozen within, never translated across. Comparability you can defend beats comparability you pretend.
  • Adaptive probes on a frozen core. Core-48 stays untouchable for comparability; an adaptive probe set spends extra measurement budget where the setup is doubtful (you fail structure → more structure). Information per minute doubles; the headline number never moves.
